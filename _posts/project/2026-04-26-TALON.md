Landing gear is carried for the whole flight but used only at takeoff and landing. TALON asks whether it can serve a second function in the air: on this simulated quadrotor the two landing-gear jaws also act as the gripper, and the vehicle flies a complete pick-and-deliver mission — approach, dock, climb, delivery — with no human at either end.

Across 384 randomized missions the pipeline completes **69.0%** of them, and the final phase records the largest share of the losses. This post is about why that per-phase count is misleading, and about the single quantity, the **grasp depth**, that the losses actually follow.

The work is written up in a manuscript with Prof. Jongsoo Choi (Soongsil University). Code and training configurations: [github.com/leesg0107/TALON](https://github.com/leesg0107/TALON).

---

## Why the Grasp

Field results say the grasp, not the flight, is the hard step of aerial delivery. At the MBZ International Robotics Challenge, one aerial team detected 14 objects, picked up 6, and delivered 4. The winner of the MBZIRC 2020 wall-building challenge placed 13 of the 22 bricks it grasped. These systems certify each grasp with a single Boolean decision and proceed on it. Even when that bit is right, every other property of the contact is left unevaluated. TALON is the instrument for asking what such a bit can conceal.

---

## The System

A mission has four phases, each guarded by an acceptance test before control passes on:

```
[APPROACH]  ──→  [DOCK]  ──→  [CLIMB]  ──→  [DELIVERY]
 RL (PPO)      analytical     analytical      RL (PPO)
```

- **Approach**: a PPO policy flies the drone to a position above the payload.
- **Dock**: an analytical PID controller aligns, descends, and schedules the close command. A PPO policy trained for the same descent proved far less reliable; the analytical controller brings 383 of 384 baseline missions to an accepted handoff.
- **Climb**: a low-gain PID controller lifts the loaded drone to 1.0 m and holds it for up to 1.0 s. The phase exists because the delivery policy was trained only on states at 1.0 m or higher.
- **Delivery**: a second PPO policy flies through three waypoints rising to 2.5 m. It was trained on a bank of 4,996 post-grasp states generated by spawning the loaded drone directly above the payload.

Everything runs in Isaac Lab 2.3.2 on Isaac Sim 5.1.0 with the PhysX TGS contact solver, 128 environments in parallel, dynamics at 300 Hz and control at 150 Hz. The drone is 1.12 kg; the payload is an 80 mm cube of 0.20 kg, 18% of the drone's mass, on a 500 mm pedestal. Each environment slot draws a mass scale, a thrust-coefficient scale, and a wind phase once and keeps them, so the dynamics vary across slots rather than across missions. The delivery target is the one quantity sampled per mission.

The acceptance test at the dock-to-climb boundary, called the interface, checks four things: 200 control steps of sustained containment, distance to the payload, tilt under 15°, and body rate under 2 rad/s. It never asks what the jaws did to the payload.

## The Geometry That Creates the Quantity

The jaws are landing gear asked to do a second job, and the geometry shows it. Held open at 50° from vertical during the descent, they are commanded to −5° at the close. At that pose the two feet would pass each other with a **33 mm overlap**: nothing can lie between them. The payload arrests the closure wherever it first resists, so a grasp is whatever configuration the jaws stop in. It has to be measured, not designed.

What the measurement shows, over 297 recorded closures:

1. Both jaws move together to a median of 31.2° and **centre the payload**: the median initial offset of 11.4 mm shrinks to 2.1 mm.
2. Then the symmetry breaks and does not reform. One jaw, the **advancing jaw**, keeps driving the payload sideways; the other is forced back to its 50° open limit. Two jaws squeezing a payload wider than their closed span cannot both advance, so the symmetric configuration is an unstable equilibrium.
3. **The same jaw advanced in all 297 closures**, although the initial offset lay toward one jaw in 138 of them and toward the other in 159. The two jaws are identical up to sign in the model, so the selection most likely lives in the contact solver's tie-breaking rather than in the physics.

The distance the advancing jaw has displaced the payload off the drone's centreline is the **grasp depth *s***, read at the handoff. A larger *s* is a deeper grasp, not a literal depth inside the gripper. Reconstructing the jaw-to-payload contact at the arrest gives an operational threshold: 143 of the 146 grasps with *s* > 25 mm have a foot against the payload (**engaged**), and 92 of the 94 with *s* < 15 mm have none (**not engaged**).

One subtlety of the baseline pipeline: the handoff commits at 200 steps of containment, but the close command fires at 225, so under the baseline rule the closure actually completes in the first half-second of the climb. The instrumented runs below defer the commit to 325 steps so that *s* is read from a finished grasp.

## The Losses Are Inherited, Not Generated Where They Are Recorded

Three evaluation runs are used, and no table below mixes them. The **baseline run** commits on the baseline rule above, launches 384 missions with a fixed seed, and runs every one to its end; the 69.0% and the per-phase losses come from it. The **handoff run** and the **closure run** are instrumented runs that commit on a *reduced rule* instead: 325 steps of containment with the distance term only and no attitude test. Deferring the commit means the closure is finished when *s* is read, and dropping the attitude test admits the full spread of arrival tilt instead of truncating it. Both instrumented runs stop at a recording target and discard missions still in flight, which biases them toward faster missions, so their group mixtures are not comparable with each other or with the baseline. The handoff run recorded 400 missions, 17 lost in the dock and 383 accepted; the closure run recorded 300, 4 lost and 296 accepted. Every result sorted by grasp depth comes from these two runs. The handoff run's 383 accepted handoffs are a different set from the 383 of 384 baseline missions that reached the interface; the equal count is a coincidence.

Sorting the handoff run's 383 accepted handoffs, all of which passed the same reduced rule, by grasp depth:

| Group | n | Median *s* | Completed | Payload lost in delivery | Tilt > 70° in delivery | Lost in climb |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Engaged (*s* > 25 mm) | 220 | 35.8 mm | **79.1%** | 4.5% | 3.2% | 13.2% |
| Mid (15–25 mm) | 55 | 20.2 mm | 72.7% | 3.6% | 10.9% | 12.7% |
| Not engaged (*s* < 15 mm) | 108 | 9.1 mm | **50.9%** | 24.1% | 19.4% | 5.6% |

A 28.2-point gap in mission completion between handoffs the pipeline cannot tell apart. The separation does not arise in the climb, which the not-engaged group actually survives more often. It arises in delivery: of the handoffs that reach that phase, 91.1% of the engaged and 53.9% of the not-engaged succeed, and the not-engaged group loses its payload in flight at 5.3 times the engaged rate. The closure geometry offers a plausible explanation for that failure mode: in a not-engaged grasp the foot does not reach under the payload, because the foot's upper face sits a median 26 mm above the payload's underside. The in-flight configuration itself is not measured, and half of the not-engaged grasps complete regardless. Over all 383 handoffs, completion rises with *s* at a point-biserial *r* = 0.29; the 28.2 points are the ends of a gradient, not an artefact of the cuts. Nor does the gap depend on the reduced rule: *s* is essentially unchanged when read half a second later, after the point at which the baseline closure completes. Had the shallow groups completed at the engaged rate, roughly 34 of the 114 non-completions would not have occurred.

The quantity is frozen early. Measured at climb entry and again at delivery entry over the 341 missions that reach delivery, the grasp depth changes by a mean of 0.055 mm, and exactly one mission changes group. A phase handed a shallow grasp has nothing to recover it with. The delivery phase records the most failures, but it inherited them from a contact event two phases earlier.

A caveat the data cannot resolve: the delivery policy's training bank was 99.2% engaged with a mean depth of 45.8 mm, while the live pipeline hands it a mean of 25.8 mm with 29.9% not engaged. The not-engaged failures therefore mix a physical component with a training-coverage component. Under either reading, the sorting quantity is set upstream and no test evaluates it.

## What Predicts Engagement

Three quantities readable at the close command are examined on the 296 closure-run grasps:

1. **Strut-axis misalignment |eₓ|.** Along the strut axis there is a **4 mm margin** between the struts' inner faces and the 80 mm payload, fixed by the geometry before any grasp was measured. Grasps within it engage in 90.5% of 42 cases, against 42.5% of the remaining 254. Fitting the cut to the data instead gives 5.5 mm, within 1.5 mm of the geometric value. This dock controller's mean misalignment is 13.4 mm, more than three times what the jaws allow.
2. **Vertical clearance h.** Weak on its own, because the best-aligned grasps are also the lowest, but above 17 mm of misalignment a grasp engages only when the clearance exceeds 49 mm. Part of the mechanism is visible in the logs: a jaw released from higher up is moving faster when the payload begins to resist, and a faster arrival leaves a deeper grasp.
3. **Initial offset s₀** along the closing axis. The sharpest separation of the three: engagement rises through 21.2%, 43.9%, and 82.8% over its terciles. Its mechanism is unclear, since the closure removes this offset before the symmetry breaks. Grasps with a large s₀ also tend to arrive with a large h, and both may be readings of one unsettled descent.

Only the 4 mm threshold was a prediction. The roles of h and s₀ are empirical observations.

## Which Quantity Governs Which Phase

Grasp depth is not the only thing crossing the interface; the drone also arrives with a tilt, for which the acceptance test does have a term. The reduced rule kept 57 handoffs at 15° or more that the baseline rule would have rejected, which lets the two be separated:

| Loss rate | Climb, tilt < 15° | Climb, tilt ≥ 15° | Delivery, tilt < 15° | Delivery, tilt ≥ 15° |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Engaged | 5.3% (n = 187) | 57.6% (n = 33) | 7.3% (n = 177) | 28.6% (n = 14) |
| Not engaged | 3.1% (n = 97) | 27.3% (n = 11) | 50.0% (n = 94) | 0% (n = 8) |

Tilt is associated with climb loss; shallow grasp depth with delivery loss, most sharply within low tilt. Among the handoffs the baseline rule would actually have admitted, those below 15° of tilt, completion is 87.7% for engaged against 48.5% for not engaged, a gap of about 39 points. The two quantities differ in one decisive respect: tilt losses can be turned into rejections by tightening a term the pipeline already evaluates, whereas grasp-depth losses have no term to tighten.

## Interventions That Did Not Work, and Why That Is the Finding

Three interventions that need no retraining, all acting at or after the close command, were evaluated as two conditions on the baseline seed:

| Condition | n | Completed | Rejected | Lost in dock | Lost in climb | Lost in delivery |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline | 384 | 69.0% | — | 0.3% | 12.8% | 18.0% |
| Shaped close + depth gate | 384 | 38.0% | 52.1% | 4.9% | 3.1% | 1.8% |
| Re-grasp (up to 3 more attempts) | 192 | 63.0% | — | 16.1% | 10.4% | 10.4% |

Shaped closing ramps the jaw command over 65 control steps instead of stepping it; gating adds the 25 mm cut to the acceptance test and ends a failing mission as a rejection; re-grasping applies the same test and, on failure, reopens the jaws and re-docks. Each condition reduced the delivery loss it targeted, and **neither raised completion**. Of the 52.1% rejected by the gate, 46.4 points failed only the new depth term. Re-grasping ended below the baseline it was meant to improve: reopening the jaws dropped the payload in 9 of 192 missions, and 22 more ran out of time. The loss did not disappear; it moved to rejections or to earlier phases.

What these conditions cannot show is whether a late intervention could raise completion in principle. Gating ends rejected missions by construction, and re-grasping discards the grasp rather than acting on it.

## What Is and Is Not Established

**Supported.** A large share of this system's losses follows a quantity produced by a contact event, carried through the next phase almost unchanged, and absent from every acceptance test. Because it is not measured, the per-phase count blames the wrong phase.

**Plausible but unverified.** Four mechanisms fit the data without being established by it. The not-engaged failures may be partly a coverage effect of the delivery policy's training bank. A deep grasp is also an eccentric load: at the engaged median of 35.8 mm the payload applies a 0.07 N·m roll moment and shifts the centre of mass 5.4 mm off axis, which would explain why engaged grasps fail the climb at about twice the not-engaged rate under high tilt, but no attitude commands were logged to test it. The retention of shallow grasps under high tilt is unexplained. And the closure has been observed under one contact solver; the instability follows from the geometry, but which jaw advances, and so part of the spread of *s*, may be solver-dependent.

**Limitations.** Everything is simulation, with one gripper, one payload, and no release modelled. The 4 mm threshold is a property of this gripper–payload pair, the 49 mm clearance value is an empirical tercile, the payload's position on the pedestal is not randomized, and the two learned policies enter as fixed artefacts.

---

## The General Lesson

A pipeline guarded by Boolean acceptance tests can be losing missions to a continuous quantity that none of those tests evaluates. The procedure that found it here is meant to transfer even where the numbers do not:

1. **Instrument** the interface where control passes on.
2. **Verify** that the phases downstream leave the candidate quantity alone.
3. **Sort** equally certified handoffs by it.
4. **Trace back** what set its value.

Three design implications follow. A continuous quality measure should accompany the Boolean test at a grasp boundary; its value is in earlier rejection and in monitoring the distribution the pipeline delivers, not in in-mission recovery. A candidate interface variable should be checked for downstream invariance before effort goes into the phases that receive it. And the consequences of a poor value should be treated separately from its production: the grasp depth belongs among the delivery policy's training-distribution variables as well.

Next steps: repeat the closure run under the PhysX PGS solver, test whether a sub-degree perturbation of the initial jaw angles redirects which jaw advances, and rebuild the delivery policy's training bank from the pipeline's own grasp distribution.
